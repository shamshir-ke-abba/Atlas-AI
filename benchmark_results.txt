   - The derivative is \( 3x^2 \) because the exponent is 3.

\[ v(t) = 3t^2 + 4 \]

The displacement \( s \) from \( t = 0 \) to \( t = 2 \) can be found by integrating the velocity function over this interval:

\[ s = \int_{0}^{2} (3t^2 + 4) dt \]

\[ s = \int_{0}^{2} 3t^2 dt + \int_{0}^{2} 4 dt \]

\[ \int 4 dt = 4t \]

\[ s = 8 + 8 \]
\[ s = 16 \]

Therefore, the displacement of the particle from \( t = 0 \) to \( t = 2 \) seconds is:
\[ s = 16 \text{ meters} \]

   \[ f'(x) = 2x \]
   
   This derivative is defined for all \( x > 2 \).

ATLAS ANSWER:
To solve this problem, we need to use the equations of motion under constant acceleration due to gravity.

### Maximum Height

The ball reaches its maximum height when its velocity becomes zero. We can use the following kinematic equation:

\[ v = u - gt \]

Where:
- \( v \) is the final velocity (0 m/s at max height),
- \( u \) is the initial velocity (20 m/s),
- \( g \) is the acceleration due to gravity (10 m/s²).

Plugging in the values:

\[ 0 = 20 - 10t \]

Solving for \( t \):

\[ 10t = 20 \]
\[ t = 2 \text{ seconds} \]

Now, we can find the maximum height using another kinematic equation:

\[ s = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \]

Where:
- \( s \) is the displacement (height),
- \( u \) is the initial velocity (20 m/s),
- \( g \) is the acceleration due to gravity (10 m/s²),
- \( t \) is time (2 seconds).

Plugging in the values:

\[ s = 20 \times 2 - \frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 2^2 \]
\[ s = 40 - 20 \]
\[ s = 20 \text{ meters} \]

So, the maximum height reached by the ball is **20 meters**.

### Total Time of Flight

The total time of flight can be found by considering the symmetry of the motion. The time taken to reach the maximum height (which is half the total time) is:

\[ t_{\text{up}} = \frac{u}{g} \]

Plugging in the values:

\[ t_{\text{up}} = \frac{20}{10} = 2 \text{ seconds} \]

Since this is half of the total flight time, we can find the full flight time by doubling it:

\[ t_{\text{total}} = 2 \times 2 = 4 \text{ seconds} \]

So, the total time of flight for the ball is **4 seconds**.

### Summary

- Maximum height: **20 meters**
- Total time of flight: **4 seconds**

These calculations are based on the given initial conditions and ignoring air resistance.

A fair coin is tossed 5 times. What is the probability of getting exactly 3 heads? Show the calculation.

ATLAS ANSWER:
To calculate the probability of getting exactly 3 heads in 5 tosses of a fair coin, we can use the binomial distribution formula. The formula for the probability \( P(X = k) \) of getting exactly \( k \) successes (heads) in \( n \) trials (coin tosses) is:

\[ P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k} \]

Where:
- \( n = 5 \) (number of coin tosses)
- \( k = 3 \) (number of heads we want to get)
- \( p = 0.5 \) (probability of getting a head in one toss)

First, calculate the binomial coefficient \( \binom{n}{k} \):

\[ \binom{5}{3} = \frac{5!}{3!(5-3)!} = \frac{5 \times 4 \times 3!}{3! \times (5-3)!} = \frac{5 \times 4}{2 \times 1} = 10 \]

Next, calculate \( p^k \) and \( (1-p)^{n-k} \):

\[ p^3 = 0.5^3 = 0.125 \]
\[ (1-p)^{5-3} = (1-0.5)^2 = 0.5^2 = 0.25 \]

Now, multiply these values together:

\[ P(X = 3) = \binom{5}{3} \times p^3 \times (1-p)^{2} = 10 \times 0.125 \times 0.25 = 10 \times 0.03125 = 0.3125 \]

Therefore, the probability of getting exactly 3 heads in 5 tosses is \( 0.3125 \) or 31.25%.

QUESTION 17/100
Category: Physics
Difficulty: Easy

What is the difference between speed and velocity?

ATLAS ANSWER:
Speed and velocity are related concepts in physics but have distinct meanings. Speed refers to how fast an object is moving, measured as distance per unit time (e.g., meters per second). Velocity, on the other hand, includes both the magnitude of motion (speed) and direction.

To summarize:
- **Speed** = Distance / Time
- **Velocity** = Displacement / Time

Displacement is a vector quantity that considers not only how far an object has moved but also its direction. Therefore, velocity can be zero even if an object is moving at a constant speed because it could have changed directions.

In simpler terms:
- Speed tells you how fast something is going.
- Velocity tells you both the speed and which way something is going.

To illustrate with an example: If a car travels 60 kilometers in one hour, its speed is 60 km/h. However, if the car turns around after traveling for half an hour (30 km), it has moved 60 km but changed direction, resulting in a velocity of 60 km/h to the right.

In summary:
- **Speed** = Distance / Time
- **Velocity** = Displacement / Time

To solve this problem, we need to consider both the conservation of momentum and the principle of elastic collision.

### Conservation of Momentum

The total momentum before the collision must equal the total momentum after the collision:

\[ p_{\text{initial}} = p_{\text{final}} \]

For a 2 kg ball moving at 10 m/s, its initial momentum is:
\[ p_{\text{ball1}} = m_1 v_1 = 2 \, \text{kg} \times 10 \, \text{m/s} = 20 \, \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} \]

For a stationary 3 kg ball, its initial momentum is:
\[ p_{\text{ball2}} = m_2 v_2 = 3 \, \text{kg} \times 0 \, \text{m/s} = 0 \, \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} \]

The total initial momentum is:
\[ p_{\text{initial}} = p_{\text{ball1}} + p_{\text{ball2}} = 20 \, \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} + 0 \, \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} = 20 \, \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} \]

After the collision, let's denote the final velocities of the two balls as \( v_1' \) and \( v_2' \). Since they are moving in opposite directions (one head-on), we can write:
\[ p_{\text{ball1}} = -m_1 v_1' \]
\[ p_{\text{ball2}} = m_2 v_2' \]

The total momentum after the collision is:
\[ p_{\text{final}} = -m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' \]

By conservation of momentum, we have:
\[ 20 \, \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} = -m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' \]
\[ 20 \, \text{kg} \cdot \text{m/s} = -2 \, \text{kg} \times v_1' + 3 \, \text{kg} \times v_2' \]

### Conservation of Kinetic Energy

For an elastic collision, the total kinetic energy before and after the collision must be equal:
\[ K_{\text{initial}} = K_{\text{final}} \]

The initial kinetic energy is:
\[ K_{\text{initial}} = \frac{1}{2} m_1 v_1^2 + \frac{1}{2} m_2 v_2^2 \]
\[ K_{\text{initial}} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \, \text{kg} \times (10 \, \text{m/s})^2 + \frac{1}{2} \times 3 \, \text{kg} \times 0 \, \text{m/s}^2 \]
\[ K_{\text{initial}} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 100 + 0 = 100 \, \text{J} \]

The final kinetic energy is:
\[ K_{\text{final}} = \frac{1}{2} m_1 v_1'^2 + \frac{1}{2} m_2 v_2'^2 \]
\[ K_{\text{final}} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \, \text{kg} \times (v_1')^2 + \frac{1}{2} \times 3 \, \text{kg} \times (v_2')^2 \]
\[ K_{\text{final}} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times (v_1')^2 + \frac{1}{2} \times 3 \times (v_2')^2 \]

By conservation of kinetic energy, we have:
\[ 100 \, \text{J} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times (v_1')^2 + \frac{1}{2} \times 3 \times (v_2')^2 \]

\[ 20 = -2 v_1' + 3 v_2' \]

Substitute \( v_1' \) into the kinetic energy equation:
\[ 100 = v_1'^2 + 1.5 (v_2')^2 \]

This gives us two solutions:
1. \( v_2' = 0 \)
2. \( v_2' = 10 \, \text{m/s} \)

If \( v_2' = 10 \, \text{m/s} \):

- Ball 2: \( 5 \, \text{m/s} \)

Both solutions satisfy the conservation of momentum and kinetic energy.

- It supports Einstein's theory of general relativity by suggesting that gravity is not a force but a curvature of spacetime caused by matter.
- It simplifies calculations involving the motion of objects under the influence of gravity, as it allows us to use Newton’s laws of motion.

A **σ bond** is a type of chemical bond that arises when two atomic orbitals overlap along the line connecting the nuclei of the bonded atoms. This means that the bonding electrons are shared in such a way that they occupy the same region around both nuclei.

A **π bond** is a type of chemical bond that arises when two atomic orbitals overlap sideways, perpendicular to the line connecting the nuclei. This means that the bonding electrons are shared in such a way that they occupy regions around both nuclei.

- **Formation**: π bonds form through side-on overlap, where one orbital's lobes point towards and away from each other.
- **Strength**: They are generally weaker than σ bonds due to their more diffuse nature and higher energy levels.
- **Examples**: Ethylene (C₂H₄), Benzene (C₆H₆).

### Key Differences

1. **Overlap Direction**:
   - **σ Bonds**: Head-on overlap, with lobes aligned along the line connecting nuclei.
   - **π Bonds**: Side-on overlap, with lobes pointing in perpendicular directions.

2. **Energy Levels**:
   - **σ Bonds**: Lower energy levels due to more localized orbitals.
   - **π Bonds**: Higher energy levels due to diffuse orbitals.

3. **Bond Strength**:
   - **σ Bonds**: Generally stronger and more stable.
   - **π Bonds**: Weaker, but still strong enough for many organic compounds.

### Summary

def factorial(n):
    """
    Calculate the factorial of a given number n.
    
    Parameters:
    - n (int): A positive integer
    
    Returns:
    int: The factorial of n, which is the product of all positive integers up to n.

Explain the difference between shallow copy and deep copy in Python and give an example where it matters.

ATLAS ANSWER:
In Python, copying objects is a crucial aspect of programming. There are two main types of object copies: shallow copies and deep copies. Understanding these differences can help you avoid unintended side effects when working with mutable data structures.

### Shallow Copy

A **shallow copy** creates a new object but references the same memory location as the original object. This means that changes to the copied object will affect the original object, because they are sharing the same underlying data structure.

#### Example:

```python
# Original list
original_list = [1, 2, [3, 4]]

# Shallow copy
shallow_copy = original_list

# Modify the inner list in the shallow copy
shallow_copy[2][0] = 'A'

print(original_list)  # Output: [1, 2, ['A', 4]]
print(shallow_copy)   # Output: [1, 2, ['A', 4]]

# The original list has been modified as well.
```

### Deep Copy

A **deep copy** creates a completely separate object and copies all the elements of the original object. This means that changes to the copied object will not affect the original object.

#### Example:

```python
# Original dictionary
original_dict = {'a': 1, 'b': [2, 3]}

# Deep copy
deep_copy = original_dict.copy()

# Modify the inner list in the deep copy
deep_copy['b'][0] = 'A'

print(original_dict)  # Output: {'a': 1, 'b': [2, 3]}
print(deep_copy)      # Output: {'a': 1, 'b': ['A', 3]}
```

### When to Use Shallow Copy vs Deep Copy

- **Shallow copy** is useful when you want the copied object to share the same data structure as the original. This can be beneficial for performance in certain scenarios.
  
- **Deep copy** is necessary when you need a completely independent copy of an object, especially if it contains mutable objects like lists or dictionaries.

### Conclusion

In summary, shallow copies are simpler and faster but may lead to unintended side effects due to shared references. Deep copies ensure that the copied object is entirely separate from the original, preventing such issues. Understanding when to use each type can help you write more robust and maintainable code.

Binary search has a time complexity of \(O(\log n)\). This means that as the size of the dataset, represented by \(n\), increases, the number of comparisons required to find an element in the sorted array grows logarithmically.

To understand this, let's break it down:

1. **Initial Setup**: Assume we have a sorted list of elements and we want to find a specific element within that list.
2. **Binary Search Process**:
   - We start by comparing the middle element of the current search range with our target value.
   - If the target is greater, we discard the lower half (the left half) and focus on the upper half (right half).
   - If the target is smaller, we discard the upper half and focus on the lower half.
3. **Logarithmic Growth**:
   - Each comparison effectively halves the size of the search space.
   - This process continues until the element is found or determined to be absent from the list.

For example, if you have a sorted array of 10 elements (\(n = 10\)), it would take at most 4 comparisons (since \(2^3 = 8\) and we need one more comparison to find the exact position).

If you have an array of 100 elements (\(n = 100\)), it would take at most 7 comparisons. This pattern continues as \(n\) increases, with each additional element doubling the number of comparisons needed.

Thus, the time complexity is logarithmic because the number of steps grows in a way proportional to the base-2 logarithm of the size of the array (\(O(\log n)\)).

    """
    Check if a given string s is a palindrome.
    
    Args:
        s (str): The string to be checked.

    Returns:
        bool: True if s is a palindrome, False otherwise.
    """

    # Normalize the string by removing spaces and converting to lowercase
    normalized_s = ''.join(c for c in s.lower() if c.isalnum())

            return False
        left += 1
        right -= 1

    return True

The physical mechanism behind this phenomenon involves several steps:

2. **Re-emission of Heat Energy**: Once absorbed, the CO2 molecules re-emit this energy back towards the Earth’s surface as infrared radiation. This process continues until it eventually escapes back into space, but some of it gets trapped in the atmosphere due to the greenhouse effect.

This trapped heat warms the Earth’s surface and lower atmosphere.

4. **Feedback Loop**: The warming further increases the amount of CO2 in the atmosphere as plants release it through photosynthesis, leading to an amplification of this process. This feedback loop is a key driver of global warming.

Explain why correlation between two variables does not necessarily imply causation.

ATLAS ANSWER:
Correlation and causation are often confused, but they refer to different concepts. Correlation means that there is a statistical relationship or association between two variables, indicating that as one variable changes, the other tends to change in a predictable way. Causation implies that one event (the cause) directly results in another event (the effect). 

The key point here is that correlation does not imply causation because just because two things occur together doesn't mean one causes the other. There could be multiple factors influencing both variables, or they might simply be coincidental.

For example, consider the relationship between ice cream sales and drowning incidents. Both tend to increase in summer months due to warmer weather. This correlation does not imply that eating ice cream causes people to drown; rather, it suggests a possible association but doesn't establish causation.

In summary, while correlation indicates a statistical link between variables, causation requires evidence of direct cause-and-effect relationship.
